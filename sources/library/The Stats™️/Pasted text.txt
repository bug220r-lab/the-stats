## 2. Audit and clean the data

Preserve all original exports. Perform cleaning in new, clearly labeled outputs.

Use reproducible Python code for substantive transformations and calculations. Suitable tools include pandas, NumPy, SciPy, statsmodels, and a charting library; choose methods according to the data rather than using every available technique.

Check for:

- Duplicate files, duplicate records, and overlapping exports.
- Inconsistent account, track, release, post, and campaign identifiers.
- Date parsing errors, mixed timezones, and incomplete reporting periods.
- Missing dates, missing values, suppressed values, and unavailable metrics.
- Numbers stored as text, inconsistent percentages, and rounded values such as “1.2K.”
- Unexpected negative values, counter resets, historical revisions, and changes in metric definitions.
- Outliers, sudden spikes, and disagreements between detailed tables and reported totals.
- Inconsistent geography, content-type, campaign, and paid-versus-organic labels.

Apply these cleaning rules:

1. **Distinguish zero from missing, unavailable, suppressed, and not applicable.** Do not automatically fill missing observations with zero.
2. Preserve the reported precision. Do not turn a rounded display value into an exact measurement.
3. Retain genuine performance spikes. Correct or exclude observations only with a documented reason.
4. Prefer verified platform exports over manual transcription when they measure the same thing. Reconcile disagreements rather than silently choosing a convenient value.
5. Use stable IDs where available. Record uncertain entity matches instead of merging them by guesswork.
6. Define deduplication keys using the source, account or entity, metric, reporting period, dimensions, and snapshot/version rules.
7. Log every material correction, exclusion, imputation, and aggregation. State how many records were affected and why.
8. Preserve quarantined records so exclusions can be reviewed.

Create a data dictionary with definitions, units, valid aggregation rules, formulas, denominators, and important caveats.

### Protect the October 20 baseline

Keep separate fields for:

- When the activity happened.
- Which reporting period the metric covers.
- When the data was retrieved.
- Whether the record belongs to the original baseline, a newer snapshot, or a documented revision.

Do not overwrite the October 20 baseline with later figures. If a newer export revises historical activity, retain both versions and explain which version each analysis uses.

The baseline date is not a substitute for the actual reporting windows in the original files.

## 3. Build comparable analysis datasets

Maintain separate tables for different units of observation, then create documented analysis views where valid joins are possible.

Depending on availability, these may include:

- Artist or account activity by day.
- Track activity by day.
- Posts and their performance snapshots.
- Audience and geography by reporting period.
- Release, promotion, playlist, advertising, and show events.
- Rolling and cumulative snapshots.

Do not force every source into one flat table.

### Metric and time-alignment rules

- Sum only measures that are additive across the dimension being aggregated.
- **Do not sum daily unique listeners or reach and label the result weekly unique people.**
- Do not sum overlapping audience segments unless the source establishes that they are mutually exclusive.
- Keep lifetime totals, interval activity, rolling-window metrics, and point-in-time follower counts distinct.
- Do not reconstruct daily activity from rolling unique-listener counts.
- Difference cumulative counters only when their scope and definition match; flag resets and corrections. Label follower-count differences as net changes, not gross new followers.
- Do not repeat an artist’s daily total across every track or an account’s total across every post and then treat those repeated values as independent observations.
- Check join cardinality, unmatched records, and row counts before and after merging.
- Use comparable reporting windows. Exclude or clearly flag partial periods.
- Preserve source timezones. Convert event timestamps when possible, but do not pretend an already aggregated daily total can be precisely shifted into another timezone.
- Use a defensible shared time basis for cross-platform comparisons and document any unavoidable mismatch.
- Separate paid and organic activity where the source allows it.
- Compare posts and releases at comparable ages, such as their first seven complete days, when the underlying data supports those windows.
- Do not assign a post’s current lifetime engagement total to its publication date as if all engagement happened that day.
- Calculate aggregate rates from compatible summed numerators and denominators where appropriate. Distinguish these from the average rate of an individual post.

Define every derived metric explicitly. For example, “engagement rate” must name its numerator and denominator.

Do not label a ratio between aggregate social views and Spotify listeners as a conversion rate without a valid attribution mechanism.

## 4. Decide which analyses the data supports

Before running significance tests, produce a concise readiness assessment for each important question:

- **Supported:** The definitions, coverage, alignment, and observation structure are adequate for the proposed analysis.
- **Exploratory:** Descriptive patterns can be examined, but inference is limited.
- **Blocked:** Essential information or a valid comparison is missing.

For each question, report the available period, unit of analysis, number of usable observations, missingness, and dependence concerns.

Do not apply one universal minimum-row threshold. Evaluate the amount of independent information, variation, coverage, and complexity required by the proposed method.

A few aggregate snapshots do not become a useful time series simply because they contain many metrics. Many tracks observed on the same dates also do not automatically provide independent evidence for an artist-level marketing effect.

Proceed with supported work without waiting for every dataset to be perfect. For blocked questions, identify the smallest additional dataset needed.

Before inspecting correlation results, record a focused analysis plan: the primary questions, outcomes, candidate predictors, comparison periods, plausible lags, and planned sensitivity checks. Label additional questions suggested by the results as exploratory.

## 5. Analyze trends and relationships

Prioritize the questions below according to actual data availability.

### A. Music and audience performance

Investigate:

- How streams, listeners, followers, saves, playlist additions, and available audience measures change over time.
- Whether growth is broad across the catalog or concentrated in particular songs.
- Which songs show sustained activity, repeat listening, or strong saving behavior where those measures are supported.
- How comparable releases perform at similar ages.
- Whether patterns differ by country, city, audience segment, or stream source.
- Whether changes coincide with releases, playlist additions, shows, promotions, or other documented events.

Use totals, medians, distributions, concentration measures, absolute changes, and percentage changes where appropriate. Show the underlying baseline when reporting percentages, especially when starting values are small.

Use moving averages for visualization when helpful, but do not treat overlapping smoothed observations as independent data for significance testing.

### B. Social content performance

Where social data exists, investigate:

- Which platforms, formats, topics, songs, and posting patterns are associated with stronger performance.
- Differences in reach, views, watch time, completion, saves, shares, comments, profile visits, and available link activity.
- Whether apparent success reflects more exposure, better engagement conditional on exposure, paid promotion, or a single unusually successful post.
- Whether older posts have an advantage simply because they have accumulated activity longer.

Keep platform definitions visible. Do not assume a “view,” “reach,” or “engagement” means the same thing across platforms.

Separate total impact from efficiency. A high engagement rate on very little reach may imply something different from moderate engagement across a large audience.

### C. Relationships between social activity and Spotify performance

If aligned observations are available, test a small set of meaningful questions such as:

- Is higher social activity associated with higher Spotify activity during the same period or afterward?
- Do shares, saves, watch time, profile visits, or measured link clicks show useful relationships with streaming outcomes?
- Are song-specific posts associated with later activity for the corresponding song?
- Do apparent relationships remain after accounting for measured release timing, promotion, paid activity, playlist changes, or existing trends?
- Are geographic patterns consistent across platforms, while recognizing that audiences cannot be matched person by person?

Specify lag direction clearly: for example, social activity on day *t* compared with Spotify activity on day *t + 3*.

Choose a limited, plausible lag set based on the data resolution and available history. Do not search every possible lag and report only the strongest result.

If only cumulative post snapshots or incompatible reporting windows are available, explain which cross-platform questions cannot be tested.

## 6. Use appropriate statistics and communicate uncertainty

Select methods that fit the question, measurement scale, and observation structure.

- Use Pearson correlation for suitable linear relationships and Spearman correlation for suitable monotonic relationships. Explain the choice.
- Inspect scatterplots, distributions, trends, and outlier sensitivity before interpreting coefficients.
- Assess temporal dependence. Check whether a relationship is explained by shared upward trends, seasonality, or overlapping reporting windows.
- Where justified, examine changes or appropriately detrended/model-adjusted series as sensitivity analyses.
- Use uncertainty methods that respect dependence, such as an appropriate time-series model or block resampling. Do not apply ordinary independent-observation tests to dependent daily observations without justification.
- For comparisons between groups, select a suitable method and report effect sizes and uncertainty. Account for repeated observations of the same track, post, or campaign.
- Use regression only when the available history and variation support it. Match the model to the outcome, keep the number of predictors modest, and check relevant diagnostics.
- Treat adjustment for measured confounders as a conditional association, not automatic proof of causality.
- Watch for relationships created mechanically by shared numerators, denominators, or components of the same total.
- Address multiple testing across the relationships, segments, and lags examined. Define the testing family and use a suitable correction, such as Benjamini–Hochberg when its assumptions are appropriate.
- Report all planned tests, including weak and null results. Do not select results solely because their p-values cross a threshold.
- Where defensible, report 95% confidence intervals, raw p-values, and adjusted p-values. Explain uncertainty in plain language.
- If model performance is evaluated, use a suitable chronological holdout or time-aware validation. Do not randomly shuffle a forecasting problem.
- Do not manufacture confidence intervals or p-values when the data cannot support valid inference.

For each substantive statistical finding, report:

1. The question and comparison.
2. Source data, date range, and unit of analysis.
3. Number of usable observations and dependence limitations.
4. Method and relevant assumptions.
5. Direction and magnitude of the effect.
6. Uncertainty and adjusted significance, where applicable.
7. Sensitivity to reasonable alternative periods, aggregation choices, or influential observations.
8. Practical meaning for the band.

Distinguish **insufficient evidence of a relationship** from **evidence that a meaningful relationship is absent**.

Do not infer person-level behavior from aggregate platform data. A relationship between TikTok activity and Spotify streams does not, by itself, identify which viewers became listeners.

Do not compare us with an “average artist” unless a credible, relevant benchmark is available with compatible definitions and a transparent comparison population.
